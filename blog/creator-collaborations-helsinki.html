How to get brand collaborations in Helsinki as a small creator

You do not need 10,000 followers to work with brands in Helsinki. You need an audience that lives here, a profile that shows it, and a way to reach the places that want those people through their door. This guide covers all three, plus the KKV labels and the tax rule most creators learn too late.

Short answerIn Helsinki, creators with 500 to 10,000 followers mostly get barter collaborations: a dinner, a haircut, a month of classes or a product in exchange for a Reel, Stories or a TikTok. They come from three places: creator marketplaces where local businesses post offers you can apply to, direct pitches to venues you already go to, and event invitations. Local audience and engagement matter more than follower count. Every post needs a clear label such as "Mainos / Saatu [Brand]", and the value of what you receive is taxable income in Finland.

1. What a small creator can realistically get

Under 10,000 followers, expect barter first and cash later. Helsinki cafés, restaurants, salons and studios trade what they sell: a brunch for two, a gel manicure, a ceramics evening, a sauna visit, a month at a gym. Cash fees usually start once you can show results or bring something extra, such as video the business may reuse in its own ads.

That matches what Finnish platforms say in public. Vaiku, a Finnish campaign platform, describes nano creators under 10,000 followers as often working "for a complimentary meal or product seeding", micro creators (10,000 to 100,000) as typically charging €200 to €500 per campaign, and macro creators €500 to €2,000 or more.[1]

Rough ranges for local businesses in 2026. Online brands and national campaigns work differently.

The useful question is "who wants my audience?", not "how many followers do I need?". A café in Vallila wants people who can walk in on Saturday. If 80% of your 900 followers live in Helsinki, you are worth more to that café than an account with 40,000 followers spread across three countries.

2. Why local businesses want small accounts

Small accounts are trusted more per follower, and for a neighbourhood business the reach that matters is the part that lives nearby. That is the whole case. Big accounts win on raw reach. Small ones win when the goal is a visit.

Research supports this. Experimental work found that micro-influencers who disclose a sponsorship can produce higher purchase intention than macro-influencers who do the same.[2] A Journal of Marketing study of 2,808 creator discount codes found that nano creators earned about €0.27 of revenue per follower against €0.05 for macro creators. The data came from a fashion retailer, so read it as a direction for local venues, not a forecast.[3] We go through the evidence, and where it breaks down, in why audiences trust small creators more than brand ads.

For you, the practical point: when a business owner looks at your profile, they are asking three things. Do your followers live here? Do they react? Will this person show the place well? Build the profile so it answers those three before anyone asks.

3. Get your profile collab-ready

Make the profile answer "who, where, what" in five seconds. A business owner opens it between two customers and decides from the bio, the grid and one Reel.

  • Bio: your city and your topic in plain words. "Helsinki · brunch, flea markets, sauna days" beats a quote or a row of emojis. Add "collabs: DM" or an email if you want to be contacted.
  • Grid: the last 9 to 12 posts should look like what a business would get. If you want café collabs, show cafés, with real light and real people.
  • One strong Reel pinned: a 15–30 second video of a place, with the name visible and a reason to go. This is your portfolio.
  • Location tags: tag the actual place and district. It proves you are local and helps the business find you.
  • Public account: businesses and platforms can only read public posts.
  • Clean history: no bought followers or engagement pods. Business owners and scoring tools can see sudden spikes and comment patterns that don't match the audience.

You don't need a media kit at this size. Your profile is the media kit. If a business asks for numbers, send a screenshot of your account insights: audience cities, age range, average Reel views over the last 30 days.

5. Using a marketplace without wasting applications

Apply to fewer offers, and pick ones you would buy yourself. A business sees your profile next to other applicants and picks the one whose audience and style fit. Generic applications to everything get passed over.

A fresh account is an advantage. If you have never done a brand post, your followers have never been sold to. Some businesses specifically look for that, because your recommendation reads as a friend's.

6. Pitching a venue directly

A direct pitch works when it is about the place, not about you. Most creator DMs fail because they could be sent to any café in the city. Owners read them between orders and ignore anything that looks copied.

Before you write, find one real fact: a dish you have ordered, a corner of the room that photographs well, something new on their account. Then keep the message short.

Weak:

Hi! I'm a content creator and I love your café. Would you be interested in a collaboration? I can promote you to my followers in exchange for a free brunch.

Works better:

Hi, I come to your Vallila place most Saturdays and your cardamom bun is still the best in Helsinki. 85% of my 1,100 followers live in Helsinki and my café Reels get around 3,000 views. I'd like to make a Reel about your weekend brunch. If you cover brunch for two, I'll post one Reel and two Stories within a week, labelled as advertising. My last café Reel is pinned on my profile.

Why it works: a real fact first, numbers the owner can check, a clear exchange, the label already mentioned, and a sample to look at. If there is no reply in a week, send one follow-up with something new (a Reel idea, a date) or move on. Never send a third message.

Good targets for a direct pitch: places that opened in the last six months, places with good reviews and a quiet Instagram, and places you already post about anyway.

7. Helsinki districts and seasons

Pitch the district your audience lives in, and time it to when venues need customers. A Kallio café gets nothing from followers in Espoo, and a terrace bar has no reason to pay for posts in July when it is already full.

Districts. Kallio, Vallila and Sörnäinen have many independent cafés, bars and vintage shops that like an informal style. Punavuori, Ullanlinna and Kamppi skew toward design, beauty and restaurants with a higher price point, where polished video helps. Töölö and Kruununhaka have neighbourhood salons and bistros with loyal local customers. Espoo and Vantaa are worth pitching if your audience is there; fewer creators work them.

8. What to agree before the visit

Agree the exchange in writing before you go, even if it is one chat message. Most barter problems come from unclear terms, not from bad faith: the creator expected dinner for two, the venue meant two dishes; the venue expected a Reel within three days, the creator planned two weeks.

Put these six things in the message thread:

  1. What you receive. "Dinner for two, drinks excluded" or "gel manicure, standard colour".
  2. What you post. Format, number and platform: "1 Reel + 2 Stories on Instagram".
  3. When. The visit date and the posting window: "within 7 days of the visit".
  4. The label. The exact wording (see section 9).
  5. Reuse. May the business repost your video or use it in paid ads? Reposting on their account is common. Using it in ads is worth a fee.
  6. Approval. Does the business want to see the post before it goes live? Agree how many changes, if any.

If a post never appears, refer back to this thread first. Most cases end there.

9. Labels: what KKV requires

Anything you post in exchange for a product, service or payment is advertising, and the label must be visible from the first moment someone sees the post. That comes from the Finnish Consumer Ombudsman's influencer marketing guideline, last clarified in November 2025.[4][5]

SituationLabel to use (examples from KKV)
You have an agreement (written or spoken)Mainos [Brand] or Kaupallinen yhteistyö [Brand]
You received a product or service for advertising, no agreementMainos / Saatu [Brand] or Saatu mainostarkoituksessa [Brand]
English-language contentAdvertisement [Brand] or Advertisement/Gifted [Brand]

The rules that catch creators out:

  • "Saatu [Brand]" or "Lahja [Brand]" alone is not enough. "Saatu" has to come with a clearly commercial word such as "mainos".
  • The label goes at the start of the caption, visible without tapping "more". In Stories and Reels, put it on screen.
  • Instagram's paid partnership tag is extra, not a replacement. KKV recommends it in addition to your own label, and it is not enough on its own if it doesn't name the company or is easy to miss.
  • A barter visit is still a commercial collaboration. No money does not mean no label.

Our guide to influencer marketing disclosure in Finland covers formats in more detail. It is a summary, not legal advice.

10. Tax: the part nobody mentions

In Finland, a meal, treatment or product you receive in exchange for a post is taxable income at its market value. The Finnish Tax Administration says this directly: the value of a service received as compensation is taxable, and its value is what you would pay for it yourself.[6]

What that means in practice:

  • A €70 dinner for two in exchange for a Reel is €70 of income in the Tax Administration's reading.
  • The €50 limit for advertising gifts does not apply to things you receive in return for promotion. It covers only ordinary promotional items.
  • Keep a simple log: date, business, what you received, its menu or list price, what you posted. A note on your phone is enough.
  • Costs directly tied to the content can be deducted, for example equipment used for it. Ordinary living costs cannot.
  • If the business did not report the benefit, you add it to your pre-completed tax return yourself. The Tax Administration compares reported income with data it receives from platforms.

This is a summary of the Tax Administration's public guidance, not tax advice. If collabs become regular income, check vero.fi/sometulot or ask an accountant.

11. From barter to paid

The move from barter to paid comes from proof, not follower count. Businesses pay when they can see that your posts brought people in, or when your content saves them money on production.

What to collect from each collab:

  • Reach and views for each post, screenshotted 7 days after publishing.
  • Saves, shares and DMs asking where the place is. These matter more than likes.
  • What the venue saw: ask the owner a week later whether anyone mentioned your post. Some venues track it with a code word.
  • Reposts by the business, which show they valued the content itself.

After five or six collabs, put the numbers in a one-page summary. When a venue invites you back, that is the moment to propose barter plus a fee, or a fee for ad usage rights.

12. Red flags

Walk away from any deal where you pay to get access to brands, where the terms appear only after the visit, or where you are asked for your password. These are the most common traps for small creators.

  • Pay-to-apply schemes. Be wary of anyone who charges you to "join a network" that has no public list of the businesses in it.
  • Password requests. No legitimate platform needs your Instagram password. Read-only access goes through public posts or official tools.
  • Unlimited usage rights for a coffee. If a business wants to use your video in paid ads indefinitely, that is worth more than a free drink.
  • "Don't label it." If a business asks you to skip the advertising label, decline. You carry the risk with KKV and with your followers.
  • Moving goalposts. "Can you also do a TikTok and three more Stories?" after the visit. Refer back to what you agreed in writing.

13. A 30-day plan

Days 1–7: profile. Rewrite your bio with city and topic. Pin your best venue Reel. Check that your last 12 posts show what you want to be hired for. Make the account public.

Days 8–14: sign up and apply. Join one or two marketplaces that work in Helsinki. Apply only to offers you would pay for yourself, in districts where your audience lives. Aim for three to five strong applications rather than twenty weak ones.

Days 15–21: two direct pitches. Pick two places you already visit. Write each message around one real fact, with your numbers and a sample.

Days 22–30: deliver and measure. Post on time, with the right label. Seven days later, screenshot the results and send the venue a short thank-you with the numbers. That message is what gets you invited back.

14. Frequently asked questions

How many followers do I need for brand collaborations in Helsinki?

Fewer than most people think. Platforms set their own minimums; on Promobeez it is 500. What decides whether a local business picks you is how much of your audience lives nearby and how they react to your posts, not the total count.

Do small creators in Finland get paid in cash?

Sometimes, but under 10,000 followers barter is the norm: a meal, service or product in exchange for content. Cash usually comes after you can show results, or for extra rights such as using your video in paid ads.

How do I find businesses in Helsinki that want collaborations?

Use a creator marketplace where local businesses post offers, pitch places you already visit with a specific message, and watch for event and opening invitations from venues and agencies. Two routes at once works better than one.

Do I need to label a barter collaboration in Finland?

Yes. A product or service received for advertising makes the post advertising. KKV's examples are "Mainos / Saatu [Brand]" without an agreement and "Mainos [Brand]" or "Kaupallinen yhteistyö [Brand]" with one. The label must be visible without tapping "more".

Is a free dinner from a collaboration taxable in Finland?

According to the Finnish Tax Administration, a product or service received in exchange for promotion is taxable income at its market value. The €50 advertising-gift limit does not apply to it. Keep a log and check vero.fi if collabs become regular.

Do I need a media kit to get collabs?

Not at the small-creator level. A clear bio, a grid that shows the kind of content you'd make, one pinned sample Reel and screenshots of your audience insights do the same job.

Is Promobeez free for creators?

Yes. Creators sign up, browse offers, apply and collaborate for free, and Promobeez takes no commission on collaborations. Businesses pay for the platform.

What if a business never answers my application?

On Promobeez an application closes after 10 days if the business doesn't pick you, and you are notified. With a direct pitch, send one follow-up that adds something new, then move on.
