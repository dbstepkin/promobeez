How to promote a restaurant in Helsinki

Everything a Helsinki restaurant can actually do in 2026, in the order it pays off: fix the Google profile, get named by AI assistants, build review velocity, work the Helsinki season, then use local creators — without breaking KKV or alcohol rules.

Demand is the problem, not capacity. In the April 2026 EK business tendency survey, the balance figure for Finnish hospitality fell to −14, down from −9 in January, and insufficient demand was still named as the biggest obstacle to sales growth.[1] Nobody in Helsinki is short of seats on a Tuesday.

At the same time, the way people find somewhere to eat moved further than most restaurants adjusted for. BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey 2026, published 11 February 2026, found the share of consumers using AI tools to discover local businesses rose from 6% to 45% in a single year, while Google's share of local discovery fell from 83% to 71%. Reviews held: 97% still read them.[2]

So the job splits in two. Be findable in the places that decide — the map, the answer, the review page. Then give people a reason to pick you rather than the other twelve places within a 10-minute walk. This playbook goes in that order, because spending on content before the profile is fixed is money paid to fill a leaky bucket.

How to use this playbook. Sections 2–4 are the foundation and mostly free. Sections 5–7 are where a small budget earns something. Section 8 is the part that gets Finnish restaurants in trouble. Do them in order.

1. Where Helsinki diners actually decide

Four surfaces carry almost all of the decision:

  • Google Maps and the local pack — the "ravintola lähellä" and "brunssi Kallio" moment, still the largest single channel.
  • Instagram and TikTok search — younger diners search inside the app, not on Google. A reel from a person they follow doubles as discovery and as proof.
  • Review pages — Google reviews first in Finland, then TripAdvisor and the booking platforms.
  • AI assistants — new, small, and growing quickly. In DoorDash's 2026 Restaurant Industry Trends Report, based on a nationally representative survey of more than 3,000 US consumers and 500+ operators, 22% said they had already used a tool like ChatGPT or Gemini to choose a restaurant.[3] That figure is American and should be read as a direction, not a Helsinki number — but the underlying behaviour arrives here with the same apps.

The practical consequence: your restaurant is being described to strangers, constantly, by text you did not write. Third-party text — reviews, creator captions, listings, a Time Out entry — is now the raw material both humans and AI systems read. You can influence it. You cannot own it.

2. Google Business Profile: the parts that actually move

Treat the Google profile as the product page and your website as support material. Most of the ranking work happens here, and most of it is free.

The three things Google weighs for local results are relevance, distance and prominence. You cannot change distance. Relevance is category and content. Prominence is reviews, mentions and consistency across the web.

The categories. The primary category carries more weight than almost anything else on the profile. "Restaurant" is too vague to win anything in Helsinki. Pick the specific one — Pizza restaurant, Ramen restaurant, Brunch restaurant, Wine bar — and add secondary categories for the other things you genuinely sell. A café that does a full lunch should say so in a category, not only in a photo caption.

Hours, including the exceptions. Special hours for Vappu, Juhannus, Independence Day and the Christmas week should go in before the day, not after the first angry review. Aggregated 2026 local-SEO analysis reports that profiles with consistently accurate hours are favoured in AI-driven local features, and inaccurate hours are one of the most common causes of negative local reviews.[4]

The menu, as structured data. Put the menu on your own site as real text, not a PDF and not an image. Add Restaurant, Menu and FAQPage schema. A PDF menu is invisible to the systems that now answer "where can I eat gluten-free in Punavuori".

Photos, weekly. Interior, exterior at night, the three dishes you want ordered, the terrace, staff. Photo views and direction requests are engagement signals, and profiles that keep adding recent photos hold attention better than profiles that were shot once in 2023.

Attributes. Wheelchair accessible, outdoor seating, vegan options, dog friendly, gender-neutral toilets. These are how filtered searches find you and how assistants qualify you against a constraint.

Posts. Weekly is enough. The lunch list, this week's special, the terrace opening. Short and dated.

Aggregated restaurant-SEO analysis in 2026 puts roughly 70% of clicks on location-based queries in the local three-pack, and reports that around 79% of restaurant searches are non-branded — "best ramen near me", not your name.[5] Treat those as directional US-weighted figures; the structural point holds in Helsinki, where most people searching for lunch have no restaurant in mind yet.

3. Getting named by an AI assistant

Assistants do not rank ten results. They name three to five and stop. Uberall's May 2026 benchmark of ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Copilot and Google AI Overviews found that 83% of restaurant locations never appear in AI-generated recommendations even though 86% are present on Google, and that the platforms apply a rating floor: ChatGPT mostly recommends businesses averaging 4.3 stars or higher, Perplexity around 4.1, Gemini around 3.9.[6]

A restaurant sitting at 4.0 can still rank on Maps and be structurally excluded from the answer. That is the single most actionable number in this article.

What the assistants read, and what to do about each:

Inputs AI assistants weigh when recommending restaurants, and the operator response.
InputWhat it means in practice
Rating level Get above 4.3 and keep it there. Below 4.1, fix the operation before spending on marketing.
Review recency A steady flow of new reviews outweighs a large old pile.
Listing completeness Hours, categories, attributes, menu, photos, phone, website — filled everywhere, identical everywhere.
Structured data Restaurant + Menu + FAQPage schema on your own site, kept current.
Third-party sources Listing and review platforms supply over 41% of the sources AI tools cite for restaurant recommendations, according to Yext data reported in 2026.[7]

One page most Helsinki restaurants are missing: a plain FAQ on your own site answering what people actually ask an assistant. Do you take walk-ins on a Friday? Is there a gluten-free pasta? Do you have a table for eight? Can we come with a dog? Write the answer in one short paragraph each, in Finnish and English. That text is retrievable in a way a beautiful hero video is not.

4. Reviews: velocity beats volume

Reviews are the highest-leverage asset a Helsinki restaurant owns, and the one most operators handle passively.

  • Ask, at the moment of pleasure. Not on the receipt. When a table says the meal was great, the host says "that means a lot — would you put that in a Google review?" That single sentence, used consistently by the floor, is worth more than any ad budget under €500.
  • Never buy or incentivise them. Offering a free drink for a review is a prohibited practice under Finnish consumer law and platform rules, and reputationally it is a bad trade.
  • Reply to everything within 48 hours. Positive included. Review response rate is treated as a ranking and trust signal, and 2026 local-SEO analysis suggests a steady flow of new reviews now matters more than raw count.[4]
  • Answer the one-star in public, resolve it in private. Two sentences, no defensiveness, an invitation to email. You are writing for the next 200 readers, not the angry one.
  • Track the rating floor weekly. If you drop under 4.3, that is now an operations meeting, not a marketing one.

5. The Helsinki year: when the money is actually there

Helsinki demand is not flat and no generic guide will tell you this. Plan the year on the pattern the city actually has.

Two things Helsinki operators underuse. First, lunch. The weekday 11:00–14:00 lounas is the most reliable revenue in the city and the least marketed — a published weekly lunch list, on your own site as text, ranks for exactly the searches office workers make at 11:20. Second, pikkujoulu prepayment, which starts in September and is over by mid-October for the good slots.

6. Districts, and why creator geography is city-wide

Kallio, Punavuori, Kamppi, Töölö, Vallila, Kruununhaka, Kalasatama and Jätkäsaari behave like different markets on price, timing and the kind of guest who walks in. Your Google categories, photos and language should match the one you are in.

Creator selection is the opposite. In a city this compact, two to four minutes on the metro makes neighbourhood-level creator targeting meaningless. What matters is whether the creator's followers are in Helsinki at all — not which postcode the creator sleeps in. A creator living in Espoo whose audience is 70% Helsinki is a better partner than a Kallio resident whose followers are spread across Finland.

Ask for the audience geography screenshot from the creator's professional account. It takes them fifteen seconds and it is the only verifiable number in the whole conversation. The full selection method is in how to choose a local creator — the ten-factor checklist there does the work, so it is not repeated here.

7. Working with local creators, without a cash budget

For a single venue, nano and micro creators with concentrated Helsinki audiences generally beat larger accounts, because a recommendation is only worth something if the reader can act on it this week. A 2026 study of 474 social-media users who follow food influencers found that trust helped explain how identification with an influencer turned into intention to visit a restaurant.[8] Revenue-efficiency research in a 2024 Journal of Marketing study of 1,698 Instagram creators also found lower-followership creators produced higher revenue per follower in a direct-to-consumer setting — directional support, not a Helsinki barter multiplier.[9]

What a barter collaboration looks like when it works:

  1. Size the offer so the creator would plausibly have bought it. A main and a drink for two people. A tasting for a review. Something with a legible price — €25 reads as €25, "a free experience" reads as homework.
  2. Ask for one thing, not five. One reel, or one carousel plus one story with the location tag. Deliverable creep is how these fall apart.
  3. Book them on a quiet service. Tuesday 18:00, not Friday 20:00. You want the kitchen calm and the room photogenic, and you want to fill a slot that was empty anyway.
  4. Give them the shot list, not the script. Two dishes you want ordered, the detail you want in frame, the tag and the location. The words stay theirs — that is the entire value.
  5. Agree reuse rights in writing before the visit. Can you repost the reel on your own account, for how long, with or without their handle. Most creators say yes if asked; almost none say yes afterwards.
  6. Judge a series, not a post. Four small collaborations over two months beat one large one, and give you enough content to fill your own feed for a quarter.

Two follow-up questions get answered in the evidence review on why audiences trust small creators more than brand ads, including the cases where a larger creator is the better call.

8. Disclosure, and the alcohol trap

This is where Finnish restaurants get caught, and it is entirely avoidable.

Disclosure. When a meal or service is given for advertising purposes, the content must be labelled as commercial. Guidance from the Finnish Competition and Consumer Authority updated in 2025 requires a clear commercial marking such as Mainos / Kaupallinen yhteistyö / Advertisement, visible as soon as the consumer sees the content — not buried at the end of a caption, not hidden behind "more".[10] The obligation sits with the creator, but a brand that asks for an unlabelled post is exposed too. Put the required label in the brief, in writing, every time. The full detail is in the Finnish influencer disclosure guide.

Alcohol. Finland's Alcohol Act was amended and the changes came into force on 3 July 2026.[11] In the parliamentary stage, the Social Affairs and Health Committee proposed that influencer marketing of alcoholic beverages be separately prohibited and that the maximum penalty payment be raised from €1,000 to €20,000.[12] Marketing of spirits above 22% remains restricted, with supervision now under the Finnish Licensing and Supervision Agency (Lupa- ja valvontavirasto), which took over from Valvira on 1 January 2026.[13] A separate long-standing rule already bans an alcohol business from using consumer-produced text or images in alcohol marketing on services it controls — which is why a bar reposting a customer's photo of a beer is a legal question, not a content question.

The operator rule that survives all of this: brief creators around food, the room and the service. Not the drinks list. If a glass appears in shot, it is incidental and it is not the subject of the caption. And confirm the current wording with Lupa- ja valvontavirasto before you build anything drink-led — this area moved twice in eighteen months. None of this is legal advice.

9. Delivery, booking, and owning the guest

Wolt and Foodora are discovery channels in Helsinki as much as delivery channels — people browse them the way they used to browse Google. Be there, keep the photography good, and treat the commission as a customer-acquisition cost rather than a margin insult.

Then build the channel you own. A direct booking link on the Google profile, a working table-reservation flow on your own site, an email or SMS list collected at the till or from group bookings. One decent email to a list of 800 previous guests announcing the autumn menu costs nothing and outperforms most paid social a small restaurant can afford.

10. Lists, press and third-party mentions

Editorial mentions do two jobs now: they bring readers, and they feed the sources assistants cite. Time Out Helsinki's 2026 restaurant list put Plein in Vallila at number one, The Alley in Kallio second and Boreal third, chosen from the editors' own visits.[14] You cannot buy your way onto that. You can make yourself easy to find and easy to describe.

Practical targets for a Helsinki venue: local food media and city guides, neighbourhood Facebook groups (as a participant, not an advertiser), a Helsinki-focused newsletter, an event listing when you do something worth listing, and a genuine relationship with two or three writers who cover the city. Send them a short note when the menu changes, not a press release when you need covers.

11. What to measure, weekly

Ten minutes every Monday, one sheet:

  • Google profile: views, direction requests, calls, website clicks
  • New reviews this week, and the running average rating
  • Bookings by source: direct, Google, platform, phone
  • Covers on the two quiet services you are trying to fix
  • Redemptions of any code word you gave a creator
  • Saves and shares on your own posts — better predictors of visits than likes

Give each creator collaboration a trackable ask: a code word at the till, a link with UTM tags, or "mention the reel". Without it you are guessing, and guessing is how a €200 barter turns into a €200 opinion.

12. A 90-day plan

Days 1–14 — foundation. Categories, hours including exceptions, attributes, 20 new photos, menu as text on your site with Restaurant and Menu schema, an FAQ page in Finnish and English. Claim and align every listing.

Days 15–45 — reviews and lunch. Floor script for asking. Reply to every review inside 48 hours. Publish the weekly lunch list as text. Set the weekly measurement sheet.

Days 46–75 — creators. Shortlist ten local creators on audience geography and category fit. Run four small barter collaborations on quiet services, each with a written brief, a disclosure requirement and a tracking method.

Days 76–90 — compound. Repost the best creator content on your own channels under the agreed rights. Pitch two local writers. Open pikkujoulu bookings if the calendar is anywhere near September. Review the numbers against day one and cut whatever did not move.

FAQ

How much should a small Helsinki restaurant spend on marketing?

Before spending anything: the profile, the menu as text, the review routine. Those cost time. After that, most independent venues get further from a rolling series of barter collaborations and a lunch push than from a small paid-social budget, because €300 of Meta ads in Helsinki buys very little and one well-matched creator reel can fill a Tuesday.

Do I have to label a meal given to a creator as advertising?

Yes, when the meal is given for advertising purposes. KKV guidance updated in 2025 requires a clear commercial label visible as soon as the content is seen. Put it in the brief in writing. This is not legal advice.

Can I ask a creator to feature our cocktails?

Be careful. Marketing of spirits above 22% is restricted, the amended Alcohol Act came into force on 3 July 2026, and influencer marketing of alcoholic beverages was proposed for a separate prohibition during the parliamentary stage. Brief around food and the room, and check the current position with Lupa- ja valvontavirasto before running anything drink-led.

How many followers should a Helsinki food creator have?

Enough to produce reach that can physically visit you. For one venue, a creator with 3,000 genuinely Helsinki followers usually beats one with 40,000 spread across Finland. Ask for the audience geography screenshot.

Is Google still the main channel in 2026?

Still the largest, no longer alone. BrightLocal's 2026 survey put Google's share of local discovery at 71%, down from 83%, with AI-assisted discovery rising from 6% to 45% of consumers in a year. Optimise the profile and the structured data, because they now feed both.

Why is nothing here about buying followers or review packages?

Because it fails on the metric that matters. Assistants weight review recency and rating consistency, platforms remove purchased reviews, and a Helsinki dining room is small enough that reputation travels by word of mouth faster than any bought number.
